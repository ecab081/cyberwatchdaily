Sky PSM Abused by North Korean Wallets: Freeze Risk Explained

Blockchain risk firm Inca Digital says wallets linked to North Korean actors, including some funded by the Bybit theft, routed about $101.9 million through Sky's Peg Stability Module. Sky was not hacked. The story matters because the module's USDC reserve sits in one address that Circle could in principle blacklist, and because it shows how stolen funds get laundered through permissionless swap contracts.

This is a laundering and exposure story, not an intrusion. Nobody has reported a breach of Sky's smart contracts. The issue is that wallets tied to North Korean actors used a legitimate, permissionless swap facility to convert freezable USDC into tokens that cannot be frozen. That same design leaves a very large reserve exposed to a single point of failure. For defenders at exchanges, DeFi front ends, custodians and compliance teams, it is a useful case study in how stolen funds move after the theft.

What happened

On November 19, 2026, CoinDesk published findings from fintech and crypto risk analytics firm Inca Digital. Inca identified about $518 million in swaps through Sky's Peg Stability Module (PSM) involving wallets linked to hacks, phishing, sanctioned entities and other illicit actors. Of that total, about $101.9 million was attributed to North Korean actors. The PSM lets anyone swap USDC one-for-one for DAI or USDS, which are Sky's stablecoins. Sky was formerly known as MakerDAO.

The headline figure is tied to the February 2025 Bybit theft of roughly $1.5 billion. Inca found that in the 10 days after the theft, wallets funded by the stolen assets routed about $91.5 million through the PSM. Nearly $30 million of that moved after the FBI publicly attributed the theft to North Korea on February 26, 2025. The FBI named TraderTraitor, a Lazarus Group cluster, as responsible. Inca's findings also reportedly include swaps linked to the Infini stablecoin theft, which Immunefi put at $49.5 million. Compliance firm Nominis separately charted more than $90 million in North Korea-linked USDC and DAI entering the PSM in late February and early March 2025. CoinDesk says Nominis verified the North Korean-linked activity at its request.

Reporting from CoinDesk and others describes what happened next:

  • After the report, money left the system. USDS market capitalization fell by more than 30% to about $3.2 billion, per Pharos data cited by CoinDesk. CoinDesk's headline says 35% while its article text says over 30%, so treat the exact percentage as approximate.
  • Most of the outflow came from one large USDS holder at an address attributed to the exchange Poloniex. That holder swapped USDS for USDC through the PSM. The attribution does not establish whether Poloniex itself initiated the transactions.
  • CoinDesk reported the PSM's USDC balance fell to roughly $1 billion from $2.5 billion a day earlier. USDS held its $1 peg throughout.
  • Ethena published a statement the same day describing how its USDe minting is limited to screened counterparties. The Sky token, SKY, also sold off sharply.

Sky's response, via the Sky Frontier Foundation, is that the flagged activity is under 0.06% of the PSM's lifetime volume, which it puts above $930 billion. It also says most of the flagged value dates from 2021 to mid-2024, and that none of it passed through its own screened interfaces. Those interfaces are sky.money, spark.fi, Grove and Osero. According to reporting, almost all of the flagged transactions arrived through third-party trading aggregators.

Who is affected

There are no affected software versions, because there is no vulnerability. The exposure is financial and operational, and it falls on these groups:

  • DAI and USDS holders. More than $8 billion of combined DAI and USDS supply relies partly on the PSM's USDC reserve for redemptions, per Inca's framing. Reported market caps were about $4.6 billion for USDS and $3.8 billion for DAI before the outflows.
  • Institutions with Sky exposure. Galaxy holds $100 million of sUSDS plus an undisclosed amount of SKY. Sky agent Grove has extended Galaxy a $500 million USDS-funded credit facility, and reporting says $375 million of loans had been financed by quarter end. Stablecoin Development Corp. reportedly held about 10% of SKY supply at the end of June 2026.
  • Aggregators and front ends. The swap flow reportedly came through third-party routing, so these services are the layer where screening would have caught it.
  • Victim exchanges and investigators. Bybit and others trying to trace stolen funds now have a documented conversion path to monitor.

The figures measure transactions by wallets Inca identified. They do not prove that every dollar came from a specific theft. Public reporting does not confirm any user losing funds, and no freeze of the reserve address has been reported.

How the attack works

This is a laundering pattern, so the usual intrusion mapping does not apply. The sequence reported is as follows:

  1. Attackers steal assets elsewhere. In the Bybit case that was the exchange itself. ATT&CK records this end goal as T1657 (Financial Theft).
  2. Stolen tokens are spread across fresh wallets. Inca says wallets funded by Bybit proceeds moved $91.5 million within 10 days.
  3. Freezable USDC is swapped one-for-one through the PSM into DAI or USDS. The PSM is a smart contract with no screening step. Per Sky, its own contracts have no freeze switch, and the independent keys on the reserve wallet were destroyed after setup.
  4. The output tokens carry no issuer-level blacklist, which makes it harder to claw the funds back.

ATT&CK has little coverage of on-chain laundering, so no further technique IDs are listed here. Mapping this to a guessed ID would be wrong.

The freeze risk

The risk Inca highlights is structural. The PSM's roughly $2.7 billion in USDC sat in a single Ethereum address, and Circle can blacklist addresses. CoinDesk reports that Circle cannot freeze only part of a balance. A blacklist would therefore lock the whole reserve, including legitimate users' deposits, and halt swaps in both directions. Because deposits are commingled, Sky cannot isolate the suspicious funds. Circle has not frozen the wallet, and no court order or law-enforcement request to do so has been reported.

Part of that risk may already have shrunk. With about $1.5 billion having left the PSM after the report, the amount sitting in the address is much lower than $2.7 billion.

IOCs and CVEs

  • CVE: none. This is not a software vulnerability.
  • IOCs: the sources reviewed do not publish a full address list. Inca's underlying report covers 422 wallets, per one outlet's summary. Defenders should get addresses from the FBI's Bybit alert, Bybit's published tracing, or a commercial analytics provider, and should not rely on secondary summaries.

Attribution rests on Inca, with partial independent verification by Nominis, and on the FBI's earlier public attribution of the Bybit theft. Sky's characterization is that the volume is small relative to the total and mostly historical.

Detection guidance for defenders

This is most relevant to exchanges, custodians, aggregators, DeFi front ends and corporate treasuries holding Sky tokens. Endpoint and email telemetry do not apply here, and the useful signals are on-chain and in compliance data.

  • Screen inbound deposits and swap sources. Check every counterparty address against sanctions lists and the FBI's published TraderTraitor addresses. Also check addresses at one or two hops from known Bybit-theft wallets.
  • Watch for swap bursts. New wallets that receive stolen assets and then immediately swap into stablecoins in large round amounts are the pattern Inca described.
  • Look at the routing layer. If you run or integrate an aggregator, log which contracts your router calls. Flagged flow reportedly came through aggregators rather than Sky's own sites.
  • Monitor reserve concentration. Treasury teams should alert on large changes in PSM USDC balance and on any Circle blacklist event for addresses they depend on.

Example hunting query (generic pseudo-query against an on-chain transaction table you ingest from a node or analytics feed):

// Finds PSM swaps made by wallets within 2 hops of a watchlisted theft address
let watch = ThreatWatchlist | where Actor == "TraderTraitor" | project Address;
let near = OnChainTransfers
    | where FromAddress in (watch) or ToAddress in (watch)
    | extend Wallet = iff(FromAddress in (watch), ToAddress, FromAddress)
    | distinct Wallet;
OnChainTransactions
| where ToContract in ("<PSM_or_LitePSM_address>")
| where FromAddress in (near)
| summarize Swaps=count(), TotalUSD=sum(AmountUSD), First=min(Timestamp), Last=max(Timestamp) by FromAddress
| order by TotalUSD desc

What it finds: wallets that interacted with watchlisted theft addresses and then swapped through the PSM, ranked by volume. Replace the contract placeholder with the PSM or LitePSM address from Sky's documentation.

Also run a retrospective check. Roughly $30 million moved after the FBI's February 26, 2025 attribution, so any pipeline that did not block those flows should be reviewed for gaps in list ingestion speed.

Mitigation and response

Priorities differ by role. In order:

  1. Treasury and risk teams holding DAI, USDS or sUSDS. Document your redemption path and what happens if the PSM is frozen. Decide in advance what exposure limits apply. Do not assume the peg guarantees instant USDC liquidity, since the PSM balance has already fallen sharply.
  2. Aggregators and front ends. Add wallet screening before routing swaps to permissionless modules. Sky's own sites screen wallets and block sanctioned jurisdictions. Third-party routes that skip those checks are where the reported flow went.
  3. Exchanges and custodians. Ingest FBI and vendor address lists with automated blocking. Plan for the case where stolen funds arrive as DAI or USDS rather than USDC.
  4. Protocol governance. This is Sky's decision, not an operator's. Inca and commentators suggest stronger screening at institutional gateways, clearer legal ownership of reserves, and defined procedures for lawful freeze requests. Public reporting does not show that Sky has agreed to any of these.
  5. Incident response. Prepare a playbook for a Circle blacklist event: halt automated swaps, communicate with counterparties, and use a legal channel to determine whether any funds can be released.

What individuals should do

Ordinary holders face market and liquidity risk, not account compromise. No credential or device-level action is called for by this story.

  • If you hold DAI or USDS, check how much of your position you would need to redeem quickly. Understand that a freeze of the PSM reserve would cut one route into USDC, not necessarily every route.
  • Use official interfaces such as sky.money rather than unknown aggregator links. Fake front ends are a separate risk.
  • Avoid trading on rumor. USDS stayed at its peg during the outflow.

FAQ

Was Sky hacked?

No. Sky says no breach occurred, and CoinDesk's reporting treats it as misuse of an open swap facility. The concern is that illicit wallets used the system and that the reserve is exposed to a freeze.

Has Circle frozen the PSM reserve?

Not according to current reporting. A freeze is a hypothetical risk, and no court order or law-enforcement request has been publicly reported.

Is the North Korea attribution solid?

The $101.9 million figure comes from Inca Digital, with Nominis separately verifying the North Korea-linked activity for CoinDesk. The underlying Bybit attribution to TraderTraitor comes from the FBI. The figures reflect wallet-level matching, not proof that each dollar came from a specific theft.

Bottom line

For SOC teams this is a lower-urgency item, with no CVE, no IOCs to deploy and no intrusion to hunt. It matters to anyone running financial-crime detection or holding Sky tokens, because it shows that stolen funds can move through open DeFi plumbing even after a public FBI attribution. The $1.5 billion outflow already shows that markets treat the freeze risk as real, even though no freeze has happened.
