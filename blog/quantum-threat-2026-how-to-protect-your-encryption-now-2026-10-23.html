Understanding the Quantum Threat to Cybersecurity in 2026

The quantum computing threat to cybersecurity is no longer theoretical. In February 2026, Google issued an urgent public warning that adversaries are actively harvesting encrypted data today, betting that future quantum computers will crack today's encryption standards. This isn't scaremongering—it's the current state of cybersecurity risk, and every organization handling sensitive information must act now.

The mathematics that underpin modern encryption—RSA and elliptic curve cryptography (ECC)—are fundamentally vulnerable to quantum computing. When a sufficiently powerful quantum computer arrives, it will render decades of encrypted communications readable to anyone with access to a quantum system. The terrifying part: attackers don't need to wait for quantum computers to become a threat. Through harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL) attacks, they're collecting encrypted data right now, knowing they'll be able to decrypt it in the future. Financial records, trade secrets, government communications, medical data—all of it is being archived by nation-state actors and criminal organizations today.

What changed in 2026 is the timeline. Recent research from Google Quantum AI in March 2026 showed that estimates for breaking RSA-2048 encryption have dropped by a factor of ten. Previously, experts believed quantum computers would need around 20 million physical qubits to crack modern encryption. Now, research suggests fewer than one million qubits could accomplish the same task in under a week. One in three cybersecurity experts now predict that Q-Day—the moment quantum computers break current encryption—will arrive before 2032.

Why 2026 Is the Critical Turning Point

The threat landscape compressed dramatically between May 2025 and March 2026. Three major research papers published within twelve months demonstrated that the quantum resources needed to break encryption are significantly lower than previously estimated. This wasn't a gradual shift—it was an abrupt realization by the security community that the timeline for a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) has shortened considerably. The Thales 2026 Quantum & AI Threat Report surveyed 3,120 security professionals across 20 countries and found that harvest now, decrypt later is now the top quantum concern, cited by 61% of respondents. Equally alarming: only 9% of organizations have a post-quantum cryptography plan in place.

How Quantum Computers Threaten Modern Encryption

Shor's Algorithm: The Mathematical Weakness

Quantum computers pose a unique threat because they exploit fundamental mathematical problems that protect current encryption. The specific threat comes from Shor's algorithm, discovered in 1994, which allows a sufficiently powerful quantum computer to factor large integers and compute discrete logarithms exponentially faster than any classical computer.

Here's why this matters: RSA encryption's security depends on the difficulty of factoring large composite numbers. A classical computer would take thousands of years to factor a 2048-bit RSA key. Shor's algorithm running on a quantum computer could accomplish the same task in hours. Similarly, ECC—which secures TLS, HTTPS, digital signatures, payment APIs, and cryptocurrency wallets—relies on the discrete logarithm problem. Quantum computers can solve this too, using the same fundamental principle.

The vulnerability is absolute. There's no partial mitigation once a sufficiently powerful quantum computer exists. Everything encrypted with RSA or ECC becomes readable.

Why Symmetric Encryption and Hash Functions Are Different

Not all encryption faces equal quantum risk. Symmetric algorithms like AES-256 and hash functions like SHA-256 only face modest threats from quantum computing. The relevant quantum algorithm here is Grover's algorithm, which provides only a quadratic speedup—effectively halving security. This means doubling key lengths to AES-256 or SHA-384 is sufficient to maintain quantum resistance for symmetric cryptography. The critical vulnerability is specifically in public-key cryptography: RSA, ECC, and Diffie-Hellman key exchange.

The Harvest Now, Decrypt Later Threat: Why the Timeline Is Now

How HNDL Attacks Work

A harvest now, decrypt later attack operates through a simple three-stage sequence that begins today, not in some distant future. In stage one, adversaries systematically intercept and capture encrypted data. They don't need to understand what they're capturing—they simply need network access at internet chokepoints, internet exchanges, or compromised access points. They collect financial records, medical data, government communications, emails, TLS sessions, VPN traffic, and SSH connections. The scale is massive: attackers collect petabytes of data per hour if sufficiently motivated.

In stage two, they store this data. Since they cannot decrypt it yet, attackers simply archive the captured ciphertext and wait. There's no immediate sign of compromise, no alert to the data's owner. The encrypted data remains in cold storage, preserved for future use.

In stage three—years or decades later—when quantum computers arrive, attackers decrypt the archived data retroactively. A patient's medical record encrypted today becomes readable. Government secrets encrypted in 2024 become accessible. Financial transactions from 2025 reveal themselves. By the time decryption occurs, the data may still be extremely valuable: healthcare information doesn't lose sensitivity, government classified data doesn't become less strategic, financial and trade secrets don't expire. This is the core of the HNDL threat: it creates present-day vulnerability for future-dated decryption.

The Present-Day Risk

Google's 2026 warning confirmed that these attacks are not theoretical—they are happening right now. Nation-state actors including China, Russia, and others are actively conducting harvest now, decrypt later operations. Criminal organizations are following suit. The practical threat is not when quantum computers arrive; the practical threat is that your data is being collected while still encrypted, and you have no way to detect that collection.

This fundamentally changes the timeline for action. Organizations don't have until quantum computers arrive. They have until adversaries finish collecting data that will still be sensitive when quantum computers become available. For healthcare records, government classified data, and long-term trade secrets, that deadline is effectively now. Nearly all long-lived sensitive data encrypted with RSA or ECC today faces significant vulnerability under this threat model.

Which Algorithms Are at Risk: A Specific Breakdown

High-Risk Public-Key Cryptography

RSA encryption in all key sizes (1024-bit, 2048-bit, 4096-bit) is directly vulnerable to Shor's algorithm. Every HTTPS connection using RSA is at risk. Every TLS handshake using RSA key exchange is at risk. Government digital signatures using RSA can be forged. Code signing certificates using RSA can be impersonated. The vulnerability isn't partial or conditional—it's complete.

Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) faces identical vulnerability. ECDH key exchange, ECDSA signatures, and all ECC variants (P-256, P-384, Curve25519, etc.) are vulnerable to Shor's algorithm. Modern TLS 1.3 increasingly relies on ECC for performance reasons, making this threat directly relevant to current infrastructure. Payment APIs using ECC become forgeable. Cryptocurrency wallets using ECC become vulnerable. Blockchain systems relying on ECDSA lose their security guarantees.

Diffie-Hellman key exchange—whether traditional DH or elliptic curve DH—is similarly at risk. Any protocol using Diffie-Hellman for key agreement faces quantum decryption.

Moderate Risk: Symmetric and Hash Functions

AES encryption and SHA hash functions face quantum threats, but not from Shor's algorithm. Grover's algorithm provides only a quadratic speedup, meaning a quantum computer running Grover's against AES-128 achieves the same security level as classical computers attacking AES-64. The mitigation is straightforward: use AES-256 instead of AES-128, and use SHA-384 instead of SHA-256. This provides adequate quantum resistance for symmetric cryptography. Most organizations already use AES-256 and SHA-384, so symmetric encryption is not the critical vulnerability.

Unaffected: Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)

Quantum Key Distribution uses principles of quantum mechanics to distribute encryption keys in theoretically unhackable ways. QKD is not vulnerable to quantum computers and not vulnerable to harvest now, decrypt later because adversaries cannot store quantum states. However, QKD is expensive, operationally complex, and difficult to deploy widely. It remains niche compared to post-quantum cryptography.

NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards: What's Final and What's Coming

The Three Finalized Standards (August 2024)

In August 2024, NIST released the first three finalized post-quantum cryptography standards after an eight-year global standardization effort. These aren't draft specifications—they're formal Federal Information Processing Standards ready for immediate production deployment.

ML-KEM (FIPS 203) is the primary standard for general-purpose encryption and key encapsulation. Formally known as CRYSTALS-Kyber, ML-KEM replaces RSA and ECC for key exchange. It achieves quantum resistance through lattice-based mathematics, specifically the Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) problem. Breaking ML-KEM requires solving hard lattice problems that remain hard even for quantum computers. ML-KEM comes in three security levels: ML-KEM-512 (NIST security level 1), ML-KEM-768 (NIST security level 3), and ML-KEM-1024 (NIST security level 5). Most organizations should default to ML-KEM-768, which provides roughly equivalent security to RSA-3072 while being faster than ECC alternatives.

ML-DSA (FIPS 204) is the primary standard for digital signatures. Formally known as CRYSTALS-Dilithium, ML-DSA replaces RSA and ECDSA signatures. Like ML-KEM, it uses lattice mathematics and the MLWE problem. ML-DSA also comes in three security levels: ML-DSA-44 (NIST level 2), ML-DSA-65 (NIST level 3), and ML-DSA-87 (NIST level 5). Organizations should typically deploy ML-DSA-65 for new signature operations. Critical infrastructure and sensitive applications might opt for ML-DSA-87. Verification of ML-DSA signatures is competitive with RSA-3072 verification in speed, making it practical for widespread deployment.

SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) is a backup digital signature standard based on hash functions rather than lattices. Formally known as SPHINCS+, SLH-DSA uses only the collision and second-preimage resistance of standard hash functions—a conservative mathematical assumption with decades of cryptanalytic scrutiny. The tradeoff: SLH-DSA signatures are significantly larger than ML-DSA signatures (thousands of bytes versus hundreds). Signing is slow (tens to hundreds of milliseconds). NIST included SLH-DSA not because it's the best option, but because it provides a non-lattice backup in case future research somehow compromises lattice schemes. For most use cases, ML-DSA is superior to SLH-DSA.

Algorithms in Development (2025-2026)

NIST has not finished standardizing post-quantum cryptography. In March 2025, NIST selected HQC (Hamming Quasi-Cyclic) as a backup key-encapsulation mechanism. HQC is code-based cryptography—it achieves quantum resistance through the difficulty of decoding random linear codes. A formal FIPS standard for HQC is in development but not yet finalized. Organizations should monitor HQC's standardization but can implement using ML-KEM immediately without waiting.

FN-DSA, based on FALCON, is also in development as FIPS 206. FALCON is a lattice-based signature algorithm offering extremely compact signatures (under 700 bytes). Its advantage is signature size; its disadvantage is that lattice-based cryptanalysis for FALCON lags behind the NIST-selected algorithms. FALCON remains in draft status as of late 2026, so organizations should prefer ML-DSA for new deployments.

The Standardization Timeline

NIST began this standardization process in 2016. By August 2023, draft standards for the three finalized algorithms were released. By August 2024, those standards were finalized. NIST's process prioritized mathematical security, implementation feasibility, and performance benchmarking in real protocols like TLS. The final selections represent consensus from the global cryptographic community, not just NIST's preferences.

How Organizations Should Prepare Now: A Step-by-Step Migration Plan

Step 1: Conduct a Cryptographic Inventory (Months 1-2)

You cannot protect what you don't know you have. Begin by cataloging all cryptographic systems across your organization. This includes TLS certificates and their key algorithms, digital signatures on code and firmware, VPN implementations, email encryption systems, database encryption, cloud storage encryption, and any cryptographic libraries embedded in applications. For each system, identify: the specific algorithm (RSA vs ECC vs symmetric), the key size, when it was deployed, and how critical the data it protects is.

Create a spreadsheet listing every cryptographic system by criticality level. High criticality includes systems protecting sensitive data that must remain confidential for decades: healthcare records, government communications, trade secrets, financial infrastructure. Medium criticality includes internal communications and non-critical services. Low criticality includes temporary data and internal testing systems. This prioritization determines your migration order.

Involve teams across your organization: infrastructure, development, security operations, compliance, and product management. This isn't solely a security decision—migration has performance implications, requires development time, and affects compliance and certification.

Step 2: Prioritize High-Risk Vectors (Months 2-4)

Not all cryptography requires simultaneous migration. TLS is typically the first priority because it protects everything transmitted over HTTPS: web applications, APIs, email, cloud communications. If your TLS is using RSA or ECC, you're exposing every user connection to harvest now, decrypt later attacks. Begin migrating your TLS infrastructure to post-quantum algorithms.

Code signing and firmware signing should be a near-term priority because signatures can be harvested and used to forge code years later. A malicious actor could compromise legitimate software through signature forgery once quantum computers arrive. Migration here prevents trust cascade failures in your software supply chain.

Long-lived data encryption (healthcare, government, finance) should be on your immediate priority list. Legacy systems that may remain in operation for decades should be prioritized higher than systems you plan to replace within a few years.

Internal communications, while important, can be lower priority than external-facing systems. Focusing first on systems exposed to external harvest attacks reduces your timeline pressure.

Step 3: Deploy Post-Quantum TLS (Months 3-9)

Modern TLS libraries now support hybrid approaches that combine classical algorithms (RSA/ECC) with post-quantum algorithms (ML-KEM). This allows gradual migration without breaking compatibility with older systems. Your TLS handshake can authenticate using both RSA and ML-DSA, and establish keys using both ECC and ML-KEM. This provides quantum safety (the ML-KEM component is safe even for future quantum attackers) while maintaining compatibility with clients that don't yet support post-quantum algorithms.

Begin with non-critical infrastructure: testing environments, development systems, internal applications. Deploy hybrid TLS certificates that include both classical and post-quantum signatures. Test thoroughly to identify performance impacts, compatibility issues, and operational challenges. After validating in non-critical systems, roll out to customer-facing applications. This typically takes 6-9 months depending on your infrastructure complexity.

Major cloud providers (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud) are beginning to offer post-quantum TLS support. If you're using managed TLS through a cloud provider, check their documentation for migration steps. NordVPN has also announced post-quantum cryptography roadmaps for VPN services, and NordPass is implementing post-quantum migration for password management—stay informed on your third-party security tool timelines.

Step 4: Update Digital Signatures and Code Signing (Months 6-12)

Migrate code signing, firmware signing, and digital certificates to ML-DSA. This prevents future signature forgery attacks. Test signed code and firmware thoroughly because authentication failures in production can be catastrophic. Implement dual-signature approaches where new code is signed with both RSA and ML-DSA, allowing verification by both old and new systems.

Update your public key infrastructure (PKI). Root Certificate Authorities and intermediate CAs should migrate to ML-DSA. This is a fundamental infrastructure change and requires careful planning. Many organizations use certificate authorities from third-party providers—check their PQC migration timelines and coordinate with them.

Step 5: Encrypt Historical Data (Months 9-18)

Data encrypted today with RSA or ECC faces retroactive decryption under harvest now, decrypt later. For long-lived sensitive data, consider re-encryption with post-quantum algorithms. This is resource-intensive—data must be decrypted with old keys and re-encrypted with new keys—but provides retrospective protection. Prioritize medical records, government classified data, financial records, and trade secrets.

This step is non-trivial. Re-encryption requires key management, database downtime or careful online processing, and verification that data integrity is maintained. Some organizations choose to accept the HNDL risk for historical data (assuming it will be decrypted eventually) and focus instead on protecting new data. This is a business decision, not just a technical one.

Step 6: Monitor Vendor Migration and Update Dependencies (Ongoing)

Operating systems, programming languages, cryptographic libraries, and third-party software all need post-quantum support. Ubuntu, Red Hat, and other Linux distributions are adding PQC support. Python's cryptography libraries support ML-KEM and ML-DSA. Rust's rustls TLS library supports post-quantum algorithms. Java, Go, and C++ libraries are adding support.

When updating dependencies and operating systems, explicitly verify that post-quantum support is included. Don't assume—check release notes. For critical security tools, test new versions thoroughly before deploying to production.

Key Takeaways: What Every Organization Must Know

The quantum threat is present, not future. Adversaries are harvesting encrypted data right now through harvest now, decrypt later attacks. Organizations have no ability to detect these attacks, no warning when data is being collected, and no ability to retroactively protect data once it's intercepted.

Timeline estimates have compressed dramatically. Recent 2025-2026 research reduced estimated requirements for breaking RSA-2048 by a factor of ten. Expert consensus now predicts a cryptographically relevant quantum computer could arrive between 2029 and 2035, with some experts predicting before 2032. The margin for action is measured in years, not decades.

Only 9% of organizations have a post-quantum plan. Google's 2026 warning highlighted this staggering gap. If your organization doesn't have a post-quantum migration roadmap, you're among 91% that lacks one. Immediate action is required.

NIST standards are final and production-ready. ML-KEM (FIPS 203), ML-DSA (FIPS 204), and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) are not draft specifications—they're formal federal standards ready for deployment. Your organization can begin migration to these standards immediately without waiting for additional standardization.

Migration takes years, not months. Testing, compatibility validation, infrastructure updates, library dependencies, and operational changes typically require 2-4 years for comprehensive organizational migration. Organizations that wait for regulatory mandates will find themselves racing against both technical and timeline challenges.

Hybrid approaches provide a migration path. Dual certificates combining classical and post-quantum algorithms, hybrid TLS using both RSA and ML-KEM, dual signatures using both RSA and ML-DSA—these approaches allow gradual migration while maintaining compatibility with systems that haven't yet upgraded.

Long-lived sensitive data faces the highest risk. Healthcare records, government communications, financial data, trade secrets—anything that must remain confidential for decades faces severe HNDL vulnerability. These should be your highest priority for re-encryption and migration.

FAQ: Answering Critical Questions About Quantum Cryptography Risk

Q: When exactly will quantum computers break encryption?

A: No one knows the exact date, which is precisely why organizations must act now. Expert estimates typically place a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) somewhere between 2029 and 2040, with recent 2026 research suggesting higher probability in the earlier part of that range. Google's March 2026 research indicated fewer than one million qubits could break RSA-2048 in under a week, tightening previous estimates. The U.S. government plans for 2035 as a planning date, the NSA requires post-quantum migration for national security systems by 2035, and some experts predict Q-Day as early as 2032. The consensus: act as if it could happen within 5-10 years, because waiting longer creates unacceptable risk.

Q: Is my organization required to migrate to post-quantum cryptography?

A: Requirements vary by jurisdiction and industry. Federal agencies in the U.S. face explicit mandates: the Quantum Computing Cybersecurity Preparedness Act requires federal agencies to prioritize post-quantum migration, with OMB providing implementation guidance. Government contractors handling sensitive information face indirect requirements through contract compliance. Healthcare organizations, financial institutions, and critical infrastructure operators face regulatory pressure to implement quantum-resistant cryptography as part of information security requirements. Even organizations without explicit mandates face business risk: customers increasingly demand quantum-safe security, insurance policies may require it, and supply chain partners may mandate it. The practical answer: you should begin migration regardless of current regulatory status, because mandatory compliance timelines are approaching quickly.

Q: Can I just wait for larger organizations to migrate first?

A: No, and this is a critical misconception. Larger organizations migrating to post-quantum cryptography first doesn't make smaller organizations safer—it potentially makes them more exposed. Attackers can focus harvest now, decrypt later operations on organizations that haven't migrated, knowing they'll be easier targets. Additionally, migration requires updates to libraries, dependencies, operating systems, and third-party software. Waiting for these upstream components doesn't eliminate your timeline pressure—it just concentrates all your effort into a shorter timeframe. Supply chain dependencies mean you'll need your vendors to support post-quantum cryptography before you can fully migrate. The longer you wait, the more you compress your own timeline.

Q: What's the difference between ML-KEM and traditional key exchange, and will my applications break if I switch?

A: ML-KEM is a Key Encapsulation Mechanism designed to replace RSA and ECC key exchange. Functionally, it serves the same purpose: establish a shared secret between two parties. Technically, it uses different mathematics (lattice-based rather than number-theoretic or elliptic curve). From an application perspective, ML-KEM and classical key exchange produce identical results—a shared secret that can then be used for symmetric encryption. Modern TLS libraries handle this abstraction, meaning applications typically don't need to change. You can use hybrid approaches where TLS simultaneously negotiates both classical and post-quantum key exchange, guaranteeing compatibility. Testing is essential because some edge cases (particularly in embedded systems or legacy code) might have implicit assumptions about key sizes or formats that change with ML-KEM. Overall, the cryptographic transition is less disruptive than the move from RSA to ECC was, because the software abstractions are now mature.

Q: Should we re-encrypt all our historical data with post-quantum algorithms?

A: This depends on your data's sensitivity and expected lifetime. For healthcare records, government classified data, trade secrets, and other long-lived sensitive information, re-encryption provides protection against retroactive decryption under harvest now, decrypt later. If you have medical data encrypted today that will still need to be confidential in 2050, re-encryption with post-quantum algorithms is justified. For short-lived data or non-sensitive information, the effort isn't worthwhile. For most organizations, the answer is: prioritize re-encrypting the most sensitive historical data, then shift focus to protecting new data with post-quantum algorithms. Re-encryption is operationally expensive (requires downtime, careful cryptographic operations, and verification), so be strategic about which datasets you target. Start with highest-value data and work downward based on resources.

Preparing Your Organization: Immediate Actions to Take Today

First, acknowledge the threat. Schedule a meeting with your security team, infrastructure team, development leadership, and executive stakeholders. Present the facts: Google's 2026 warning, the HNDL threat, the compressed timeline, and the standardized algorithms from NIST. Make clear that this isn't optional speculation—it's an active threat being exploited by adversaries right now.

Second, assign responsibility. Designate a post-quantum cryptography program lead or working group. This person or team owns the migration roadmap, coordinates across departments, tracks progress, and escalates blockers. Without clear ownership, migration efforts become diffuse and stall.

Third, begin your cryptographic inventory in parallel. Document what you have, prioritize by risk, and create timelines. Don't let perfect be the enemy of good—start broad and refine as you learn.

Fourth, engage your vendors and infrastructure providers immediately. If you use managed TLS through a cloud provider, ask about their post-quantum migration roadmap. If you depend on cryptographic libraries, check their PQC support timelines. If you use third-party security tools like password managers or VPNs, confirm they have post-quantum migration plans. Vendor timelines often become your bottleneck, so identify and communicate with them early.

Fifth, plan your testing. Post-quantum cryptography is new to most production environments. Set up testing infrastructure where you can experiment with hybrid approaches, measure performance impacts, and validate compatibility before production deployment.

Finally, prepare your compliance and governance. If you have audit requirements, regulatory obligations, or customer contractual demands around encryption standards, begin documenting how post-quantum migration satisfies those requirements. Compliance approval can move faster than technical implementation, so start early.

Conclusion: Acting on the Quantum Threat in 2026

The quantum computing threat to cybersecurity is no longer a theoretical future concern—it's an active present-day vulnerability. In 2026, the convergence of accelerating quantum computing progress, publicly confirmed harvest now, decrypt later attacks, and finalized post-quantum cryptography standards from NIST creates an unprecedented moment. For the first time, organizations have both the threat clarity and the technical solutions required to act. The question is not whether to migrate to post-quantum cryptography, but how quickly.

Quantum computers might not arrive until 2035, or they might arrive in 2030. Recent algorithmic breakthroughs and hardware roadmaps suggest the later timeline is increasingly implausible. But even if quantum computers don't arrive for a decade, the harvest now, decrypt later threat means attackers have already begun collecting the data your organization produces today. Every encrypted communication, every encrypted file, every encrypted database record may already be in an adversary's archive, waiting for quantum computers to decrypt it.

The most important quantum computing action isn't waiting for quantum computers to become real—it's migrating to post-quantum cryptography before data of lasting value gets harvested. For healthcare data, government information, financial records, and trade secrets, that deadline is now. NIST's finalized standards (ML-KEM for encryption, ML-DSA for signatures, SLH-DSA as a conservative backup) are production-ready and can be deployed immediately. Cloud providers, cryptographic libraries, and operating systems are adding support. The technical capability to migrate exists.

What's missing is organizational action. Only 9% of organizations have a post-quantum migration plan. Ninety-one percent are exposed. Your organization should immediately conduct a cryptographic inventory, prioritize high-risk systems, begin testing hybrid post-quantum/classical approaches, and establish a multi-year migration roadmap. The effort is significant—comprehensive migration typically takes 2-4 years—but the alternative is data harvested today becoming readable tomorrow.

The quantum computing threat in 2026 isn't arriving in some distant future. It's happening now, in the form of attackers collecting encrypted data they can't yet decrypt but will be able to decrypt eventually. The opportunity to protect your organization isn't years away. It's this quarter, this year, this month. Organizations that migrate to post-quantum cryptography quickly will be protected. Organizations that wait will discover that the most valuable data they protected was already collected, and all that remains is the certainty of eventual exposure.
